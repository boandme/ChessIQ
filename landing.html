How it works

Three steps, then the part that teaches you.

01

Look at the position

Real games, drawn from tournament play and from Lichess. Nothing is constructed for the lesson, so you are reading the same awkward middlegames you get over the board.

02

Make the call

White is better, Black is better, or it is level. One judgement, no move to find. Being close still counts: reading a position as level when it sits a tenth of a pawn past the line costs you far less than reading it backwards.

03

Find out why

Stockfish gives the verdict. A written breakdown gives the reasoning: which imbalance decides it, the mistake most players make here, the move the engine wants and the plan behind it.

Why it matters

Calculation finds the move. Judgement picks the plan.

Most chess training asks you to calculate. Find the fork, find the mate in three. That skill is worth having, but it only applies on the handful of moves where a tactic actually exists.

The rest of the game runs on judgement. Is my structure worse than it looks. Is their king genuinely safer. Do I have time for this plan, or am I already defending. None of those have a forcing answer, and there is no engine at the board to settle them.

Players who can evaluate a position choose better plans, trade into better endings, and know when to hold instead of push. It is the difference between knowing an opening and understanding one.

  • You get the reasoning, not a number An evaluation bar tells you that you were wrong. ChessIQ tells you which feature of the position you misread.
  • The difficulty follows you Start by counting material. Finish on positions decided by one weak square or a single tempo.
  • Missed positions come back Anything you get wrong returns later, once enough has happened in between that you are recalling it rather than remembering it.

Ranked

Then prove it holds up under a clock.

Training is untimed on purpose. Ranked is not. Ten positions, fifteen to thirty seconds each depending on difficulty, and no explanation at all until the run is over.

Play solo against two randomly drawn themes, or head to head against another player on the same ten positions at the same time. Both feed a separate rating and a seasonal ladder running from Pawn to Grandmaster.
